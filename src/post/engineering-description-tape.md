# The Description Tape: A Theory of AI-Era Software Engineering

Decay has stalked software since the field began. Documentation drifts from implementation, conventions erode, and codebases grow faster than the collective understanding of them. Fred Brooks named this fifty years ago: "Systems program building is an entropy-decreasing process, hence inherently metastable. Program maintenance is an entropy-increasing process, and even its most skillful execution only delays the subsidence of the system into unfixable obsolescence."[^brooks] A built system holds its order only temporarily, and only under pressure. Before AI coding agents, teams fought this entropy tooth and nail — through mentorship, careful hiring, code review, and documentation discipline — exhausting work that could slow decay but never reverse it. What kept systems from outright collapse was external forcing: customer obligations, revenue, deadlines, market position. The decay of pre-AI engineering was propped up from outside and, at best, slowed from within.

Something has changed, and it has split the field in two. Many engineering teams are accelerating as AI models and tooling improve. Many others stagnate or even decelerate, despite access to the same models and the same tooling. The 2025 DORA report — Google's survey of roughly a hundred thousand practitioners — found that AI functions as an amplifier, magnifying the strengths of high-performing organizations and the dysfunctions of struggling ones, widening the gap between them rather than closing it.[^dora] However disparate the two outcomes look, both are expressions of a single structural change.

That change is this: for the first time, the description of *how software gets built* can be externalized into an artifact that machines read and act on directly. AI coding agents produce software from product specifications, conventions, software practices, customer stories, and the tribal knowledge that used to live only in human heads. This externalized description is a new kind of object, and it makes a previously uncrossable threshold crossable. On one side, the loop that produces software can now also maintain the description of how to produce it — a genuinely self-sustaining equilibrium in which each cycle leaves the description sharper, velocity compounds, and engineering effort shifts toward the loop itself. On the other side, the same tools accelerate decay, producing more code, faster, against a description that degrades underneath it. The two regimes are not better-and-worse versions of the same thing. They are different in kind. AI did not introduce decay; decay was always the default. What AI introduced is the possibility of escaping it — and which regime a team lands in is now the most consequential fact about it.

## Architecture: The Description Tape

The description that AI-era engineering operates on has a precise structural counterpart in the theory of self-replicating systems.

In the 1940s, John von Neumann worked out how a machine could build a copy of itself.[^vn] The architecture requires four parts: a constructor that reads instructions and builds the thing described; a copier that duplicates the instructions verbatim without interpreting them; a controller that coordinates the two; and the instructions themselves — the description tape. The tape plays two roles. Read *as instructions*, it gets interpreted and used to build. Copied *as data*, it gets propagated forward unchanged. This separation between description and interpretation is exactly what prevents the infinite regress that would otherwise doom any system trying to reproduce itself: the description does not have to describe itself describing itself; it just has to describe the machinery, while a separate copying operation handles its own propagation. The same architectural insight shows up in DNA, in stored-program computers, in autocatalytic chemistry, and in any system that reproduces or extends itself through descriptions. Separate the description from the interpretation, and self-reference becomes possible.

It is tempting to say pre-AI engineering had no description tape at all. That is not quite right, and the precise version of the claim is more interesting. Pre-AI engineering had a description tape for the *formalizable* fraction of its knowledge — and that fraction compounded for fifty years. Source code read by a compiler is exactly an externalized description, mechanically interpreted by a constructor. So are type systems, linter configurations, schemas, and CI pipelines: process descriptions, externalized, version-controlled, and genuinely load-bearing. The whole history of the field — assembler to compiler to frameworks to open source — is the story of that formal fraction compounding.

What pre-AI engineering never had was a tape that covered the *tacit* remainder: the conventions, the abstractions, the rationale behind decisions, the accumulated judgment about what works in this codebase, the sense of what the system should become. Peter Naur identified this remainder precisely in 1985. He argued that a program is not its source code but a *theory* held in the minds of the people who built it; that this theory cannot be reconstructed from code and documentation alone; and that when the team holding it disperses, the program dies — it still runs, but "demands for modifications cannot be intelligently answered."[^naur] Naur was right, and his claim held for forty years. The theory could not be externalized. It lived in heads, in mentorship, in oral tradition, in retros, and in documentation that was necessary but never sufficient. It transferred from person to person at the bandwidth of conversation and apprenticeship, and it walked out the door when people left.

The line between the formal fraction and the tacit remainder was the *formality boundary*: a description had to be formal — parseable, compilable, executable — to be load-bearing, and only the formalizable part of engineering knowledge could get onto the tape. This is what AI changes, and it is the single structural fact the rest of this essay builds on. AI agents make informal, natural-language description executable. The boundary moves. The tape can now hold the judgment-laden remainder that Naur said could only live in human heads. The compiler was the previous categorical change of this kind — it turned the human skill of translating algorithms into machine instructions into something a machine could do from a written description. The agent is the next one. AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md files, skills documentation, conventions documents, agent configurations, evaluation suites, schemas, and architecture diagrams are the substrate the construction machinery now reads. This is not a slightly better version of documentation. It changes what kind of object the engineering loop operates on.

Mapping von Neumann's architecture onto current engineering: the agents are the constructor, reading instructions and producing software. Version control is the copier, propagating the tape forward across loops without interpreting it. The workflow — ticket management, code review, deployment — is the controller, coordinating when construction happens and when the tape gets updated. The externalized substrate is the tape. And the codebase is the output.

The codebase is what the constructor produces. The description tape is the constructor's substrate. They play categorically different roles in the architecture, they evolve under different dynamics, and they require different kinds of work to maintain. Confusing them — treating the tape as just more code, or treating the codebase as the only output that matters — is the most common conceptual error in current discussions of AI-era engineering.

## Compounding: Why the Formal Fraction Always Won

Before going further, it is worth being precise about *why* the formal fraction compounded while the tacit remainder decayed — because the same mechanism explains what happens when the boundary moves.

Compounding means the output of one round becomes leveraged input to the next: gains multiply instead of add. For knowledge to compound, it has to have three properties. It must be **executable without a human interpreter**, so a machine can act on it directly and its quality determines output. It must be **copyable at zero marginal cost**, so that once written it propagates to every user without being re-derived — what economists call a nonrival good, the property Paul Romer identified as the engine of increasing returns and long-run growth.[^romer] And it must be **composable**, so each layer can be built using the leverage of all the layers below it — the combinatorial evolution by which, as Brian Arthur describes it, new technologies are assembled from existing ones that then become building blocks for the next.[^arthur] The first property is exactly von Neumann's tape read as instructions; the second is the tape copied as data; the third is what lets tapes stack.

Formal artifacts have all three properties. Tacit knowledge has none. A formal description is *capital*: written once, it yields forever. Tacit knowledge is *labor*: consumed in each use, re-paid for each new person, depreciating constantly and lost entirely when its holder leaves. This is the whole difference.

Read the lineage through this lens. Assembler took the human skill of mapping mnemonics to machine code and wrote it down as a program; nobody re-derives it, everyone builds on it. The compiler formalized the craft of translating algorithms into instructions — and then compilers compiled themselves, the constructor reading a description of the constructor, von Neumann's closure achieved in tooling decades ago. Frameworks and libraries wrote each solved problem down once for reuse by millions; the web server that took months of C in 1980 is an import statement today. Open source and version control became the copier at industry scale, propagating formal descriptions verbatim to everyone. Each layer raised the level of abstraction at which the next was written, which is why the gains were multiplicative. A single developer today wields the accumulated leverage of every layer below — not because people got smarter, but because fifty years of formal descriptions compounded underneath them.

This resolves an apparent paradox. How could software decay by default for fifty years, exactly as Brooks said, while the industry compounded spectacularly? Both are true, and the formality boundary is why. Compounding happened wherever descriptions could be made formal — and it happened *globally*, across the whole industry. Decay happened wherever they could not — and it happened *locally*, inside every organization, no matter how good. The heroic practices of the best engineering cultures were all labor performed on the tacit side of the line, because nothing there could cross it. AI's claim, in one sentence, is that the line just moved: it does to the tacit remainder what the compiler did to algorithmic skill.

## The Pre-AI Equilibrium: Why Decay Was Always the Default

The intro claimed that pre-AI engineering was always a decay process, held in place by external forcing rather than internal self-sustainment. This section develops the claim, because it changes the meaning of what AI has done. The bifurcation we observe today — between organizations accelerating and organizations producing increasingly incoherent output — is not engineering becoming better for some and worse for others. It is engineering becoming, for the first time, capable of crossing a threshold that was previously uncrossable. Some organizations cross it. Most do not.

To see why the threshold was uncrossable, it helps to make two concepts concrete: *closure* and *external forcing*.

A loop has **closure** when every part of the description it consumes is produced by some step of the loop itself. The concrete test is an audit: list everything construction reads from to do its work — conventions, architectural decisions and their rationale, the definition of quality, the agent configurations, domain knowledge. For each item, name the step of the loop that keeps it true. If a part is refreshed as a byproduct of the work itself — a review step that updates the convention doc whenever it catches a violation — that part is inside the loop. If a part stays current only because a senior engineer remembers it, or a new hire is trained into it, or a manager notices it drifting, that part is outside the loop. Closure holds when no part depends on out-of-loop human memory to stay current. One hole, and the loop is leaking that part to entropy.

**External forcing** is what patches the holes. Deadlines, revenue, customers, and competitive pressure do not act on the codebase directly; they act *through humans*, compelling human attention onto exactly the parts the loop is not maintaining on its own. A customer escalation forces an engineer to re-understand and fix a module the description no longer describes correctly. A quarterly launch forces enough cleanup to keep a decaying area shippable. The crucial point is that the patch is *consumed*, not banked: fixing the module today does not update the description, so the same hole reopens, and next quarter's deadline has to force the fix again. The system keeps functioning, but it pays full price every loop, forever.

Now the pre-AI equilibrium is easy to state. The tacit majority of the description could not be externalized, so most of it lived in human heads, where it could not be a part the loop produces. The holes were permanent. External forcing was the only available substitute, and external forcing does not compound — it is labor, re-paid every cycle. No matter how good an engineering culture was, the loop that produced software could not also produce the description of how to produce software in any persistent form. The substrate was always being replenished by external inputs: hiring, training, mentorship, leadership attention.

The great engineering cultures are best understood not as exceptions to this but as the most effective patching systems ever built. Early Google institutionalized mentorship at scale: its "readability" process is a standardized, company-wide system for propagating conventions through code review, and its governing idea — software engineering as "programming integrated over time" — is the decay-fighting thesis stated from inside the org.[^google] Jane Street treated reading code as a risk-management discipline; early on, senior traders committed to reading every line of code entering the core trading systems, and the firm runs an explicit apprenticeship model.[^janestreet] The most extreme case on record is NASA's on-board shuttle software group: its last three 420,000-line releases had one error each, achieved by making process cost dwarf construction cost — a 6,366-line GPS upgrade required 2,500 pages of specification.[^nasa] All of this worked, sometimes for decades. But it was exhausting; it did not scale past the bandwidth of human relationships; and it was fragile to exactly the shocks that remove the forcing — leadership transitions, fast growth, acquisitions. Human substrate cannot compound, because knowledge transferred person-to-person is re-paid every time rather than banked. Even these slow-decay regimes were decay regimes. The question was only how fast.

The biological parallel clarifies the structure. Pre-life chemistry on early Earth was full of complex molecules — amino acids, simple polymers, lipid membranes — that persisted not because they sustained themselves but because external energy kept producing them. Geothermal gradients, ultraviolet radiation, lightning, tidal cycles: these were forcing terms that drove chemistry uphill against entropy. Stop the forcing, and the molecules decay back toward equilibrium with their surroundings. Most chemistry, on most of the Earth, throughout most of its history, has been this kind of externally driven persistence.

Life is what happened when chemistry crossed a threshold — not the molecules getting more complex, but a structural property emerging: autocatalytic closure. In the formalism developed by Stuart Kauffman, Mike Steel, and others studying the origin of life, a reaction network is autocatalytic if every catalyst the set needs is produced by some reaction in the set.[^raf] The system makes what it needs to make more of itself. Once closure is achieved, the network is self-sustaining: it no longer requires continuous external forcing to maintain its own components, because the components are produced by its own activity. The threshold between externally driven chemistry and autocatalytic chemistry is sharp. Either closure holds or it does not.

The engineering analog is direct, not metaphorical, and it is the same closure defined above. Pre-AI engineering operated entirely on the externally driven side of the threshold. The description couldn't be externalized in any way that scaled — humans don't scale as substrate, and oral tradition has hard bandwidth limits — so the autocatalytic threshold was effectively uncrossable. AI changes the picture because the description can now be externalized. The tape exists. Every agent can read it. It can be improved, version-controlled, refined, and kept coherent. For the first time, an engineering organization has the structural ingredients for autocatalytic closure: a substrate the loop both produces and uses, maintained by the loop itself rather than only by external forcing.

The threshold is crossable. Some organizations are crossing it. Most are not.

## Dynamics: The Joint Recurrence and Its Asymmetric Attractors

The pre-AI development loop was a one-dimensional recurrence:

cbᵢ₊₁ = D(cbᵢ)

The codebase at step i+1 is produced by applying a development process D to the codebase at step i. The state space is one-dimensional: one thing evolves, the codebase. D itself is exogenous and constant within the dynamics of the loop. Whatever shapes D — hiring, organizational learning, tooling investment — happens on a slower, separate timeline that the inner loop does not see.

The AI-era loop is a two-dimensional joint recurrence:

(Dᵢ₊₁, cbᵢ₊₁) = F(Dᵢ, cbᵢ)

The state at step i+1 is a pair: the next description tape and the next codebase. Both are produced by applying a joint update operator F to the previous pair. The state space has been lifted: two things now evolve, in parallel and in mutual dependence. The description shapes what gets built; what gets built shapes the next description. They are no longer separate timelines. They are coupled in the inner loop. This shift from a one-dimensional to a two-dimensional recurrence is the formal expression of the architectural change — the mathematics has acquired a second variable with its own dynamics, and the lifted state space is where an asymmetric attractor structure becomes possible.

Define the rails coefficient as the ratio of description-tape quality across one loop:

r = q(Dᵢ₊₁) / q(Dᵢ)

Here q is a scalar measure of substrate quality: how coherent the tape is, how well it captures the real conventions of the system, how reliably it leads agents to produce work that fits the codebase. The function q is not currently measurable in any precise way, and the theory is honest about that. What matters for the predictions is not the value of q but the behavior of r — whether it is greater than or less than 1. If r > 1, the tape got sharper across the loop. If r < 1, it got duller. Note that r > 1 is just closure holding, expressed as a number: the substrate is regenerated faster than it decays. r < 1 is one or more holes leaking.

Velocity rides on substrate quality. The speed at which one application of F completes — how fast an agent produces a coherent next state given the current one — depends on how good the description is that the agent reads. Better tape, faster loop:

vᵢ ∝ q(Dᵢ)

Time per loop is the inverse of velocity, and total wall-clock time over n loops is the sum of those inverses:

T(n) = Σ 1/vᵢ

This setup produces two regimes, asymmetric in their stability.

In the **self-sustaining regime**, r > 1. The substrate compounds. q(Dₙ) grows. Velocity rises. Cumulative time T(n) converges — each loop is faster than the last, and the total time to run arbitrarily many loops is bounded. The system is internally stable: small perturbations, like an agent making a mistake or a convention drifting, are corrected by the system's own activity. Closure holds. The substrate is produced by the loop that uses it.

In the **decay regime**, r < 1. The substrate erodes. q(Dₙ) falls. Velocity drops. Cumulative time T(n) diverges — each loop is slower than the last, and total time grows without bound. Without external forcing, the trajectory ends in dissolution: the substrate becomes too incoherent for agents to produce useful work, and the loop fails. But real organizations rarely experience pure dissolution, because external forcing exists. Customers expect software. Revenue depends on it. Deadlines exist. These forces stabilize the system at a survival floor — functioning and producing output, but at much lower quality and much slower velocity than the self-sustaining regime. This is the stagnation steady state: practically persistent, sometimes for years, but not internally self-sustaining. Remove the forcing and it collapses.

This is exactly Brooks's metastability, made dynamical. A built system, he said, is "inherently metastable": its order is a local resting point that holds only until something disturbs it, like supercooled water that stays liquid below freezing until a single speck of dust flashes it to ice. The decay regime is that metastable state — locally persistent, externally propped, with no internal force restoring its order. The self-sustaining regime is the genuinely different thing Brooks could not have: a state with its own restoring dynamics, where perturbations are corrected from inside. Brooks identified the metastability in 1975. The claim of this essay is that AI makes a *truly* stable attractor reachable for the first time.

The two attractors are asymmetric. The self-sustaining one is genuinely stable; its persistence comes from inside the loop. The decay one is internally unstable but externally propped; its persistence comes from forces outside the engineering work. The gap between them is not merely quantitative. Organizations in the two regimes do categorically different work, produce categorically different artifacts, and operate under categorically different cost structures.

The same object can be seen three ways, and the alignment is the reason the framework is load-bearing rather than decorative. The self-sustaining regime is *autocatalytic closure* (the mechanism: the loop produces its own substrate), a *region of tape-space closed under F* (the invariant: applying the loop keeps you in the coherent region), and a *stable attractor* (the dynamics: nearby states are pulled in). Below the threshold, none of the three holds: the loop leaks its substrate, F drifts you out of the coherent region, and persistence depends entirely on external forcing — market pressure, customer obligations, human attention spent fighting fires.

The predictions live in this structural asymmetry, not in any numerical value of r. We cannot currently compute r for a real organization. But we can recognize which regime an organization is in by its symptoms — and the symptoms are categorically distinct, which is why this matters in practice.

## Labor: The Categorical Reclassification of Human Work

The architectural change has a direct consequence for human labor. Construction work — writing code, implementing features, generating tests — moves to agents and scales with compute rather than engineer-hours. What remains for humans is substrate labor: maintaining the description tape, curating conventions, designing evals, exercising architectural judgment. This work behaves more like capital formation than ongoing labor, and it has reclassified what "engineer" means in a way the field and its institutions have not yet absorbed.

Pre-AI, human labor was distributed across the entire construction process. Engineers wrote the code, reviewed the code, maintained the implicit description through mentorship and retros, and operated the workflow through planning and coordination. The same humans did all four kinds of work, often in the same week. Construction labor dominated in volume, and the cost structure of software was roughly linear in features shipped: more features required more engineer-hours, because engineer-hours were the actual input to construction.

AI-era, the labor profile splits into two kinds of work that differ in their economic character.

**Construction labor** — writing code, generating tests, implementing features, executing the steps of an agreed-upon plan — is performed by agents. Its cost scales with compute: agent count, model size, context length, inference time. Adding throughput is a matter of paying more for compute, not hiring more humans. The marginal cost of construction is approaching the marginal cost of electricity.

**Substrate labor** — maintaining the tape, curating conventions, designing evaluation suites, evaluating agent output at the substrate level rather than the feature level, exercising judgment about what the system should become — is performed by humans. It does not scale with features shipped. It scales with the complexity and rate of change of the system being described. Two organizations shipping the same volume of features can require radically different amounts of substrate labor depending on the inherent complexity of what they build.

These two kinds of labor differ on four axes. Construction **scales with output**: ten features take roughly ten times the construction. Substrate **scales with system complexity**: ten features in a well-described system take roughly the same substrate labor as one. Construction is **reproducible**: an agent writes the hundredth React component much like the first. Substrate is **judgment-bound**: deciding whether a pattern is worth codifying depends on long-term context about where the codebase is going, and cannot be replicated by a process. Construction has **clear inputs and outputs**: a description and a request go in, a merged change comes out. Substrate is **meta-work**: the output of an hour improving the tape is that *future construction goes better*, with returns appearing downstream, distributed across many later loops. And construction is **hourly labor**, while substrate is **capital investment**: an hour spent writing a precise convention propagates through every subsequent agent loop that reads it; a well-designed eval catches a class of regressions for years; a clean architectural decision shapes hundreds of future choices.

There is a precise economic way to say this. The reason substrate labor is capital and construction labor is not comes back to nonrivalry. Pre-AI, the tacit description was a *rival* good: a senior engineer's attention is consumed by whoever is using it, which is exactly why mentorship does not scale. Externalizing the tape converts the description from a rival good — human attention — into a nonrival one — text that any number of agents read at zero marginal cost. The hour spent on the tape is no longer consumed in the production of one feature; it is invested in a nonrival substrate that produces returns across all future loops. This is a real reclassification, not a stylistic relabeling: pre-AI engineering work was mostly labor in the economic sense, consumed in production and scaling linearly with output; AI-era engineering work is mostly capital formation.

Several consequences follow.

**Team size decouples from output.** If construction is cheap and substrate is the bottleneck, small teams with excellent substrate practice can out-produce much larger teams with mediocre substrate practice. This is already happening: AI-native startups report revenue-per-employee multiples that would have been impossible under pre-AI economics, with very small teams sustaining products that once required large headcounts.[^tinyteams] The bottleneck has moved, and it is not labor volume.

**The role of "engineer" has structurally shifted.** Pre-AI, an engineer was someone who constructs software; the central activity was writing code, and everything else was an adjunct. AI-era, an engineer is someone who maintains the description of how software is constructed; the central activity is curating the substrate, and construction is delegated. The word survives the transition, but the role it denotes is different.

**Senior judgment has gotten more valuable, not less.** The naive expectation was that AI would compress the value of senior engineers by automating their work. The opposite has happened. Substrate work is judgment-heavy, and senior engineers are where judgment lives. The work AI replaces is more often junior construction than senior architectural judgment. Seniors who can curate substrate well have become more leveraged, not less.

**The skills that matter shift.** Pre-AI: write code quickly and correctly, debug efficiently, hold large amounts of system context in working memory. AI-era: describe systems clearly in writing, design evals that detect what actually matters, recognize when an implicit convention should be codified, exercise taste about what the system should become. The first set is increasingly automated. The second is human-bound and in short supply.

One honest open question runs through all of this. The reclassification from labor to capital formation is structural, but the *total volume* of substrate labor required across the industry is empirical and uncertain. It could go either way. Cheaper construction could enable far more software to exist, requiring more substrate to maintain it — a Jevons-paradox expansion. Or the leverage of small teams could compress total engineering employment, concentrating work in a smaller number of high-judgment roles. The theory predicts the categorical shift. It does not predict the volume.

## Predictions and Observable Signatures

A theory of asymmetric attractors that did not manifest in observable bifurcation would be weak. This theory predicts that engineering organizations should be visibly diverging into two qualitatively different kinds of system. The contemporary observations of acceleration and stagnation that opened this essay are evidence for that prediction, not the foundation the theory rests on. The theory makes four specific predictions, each checkable against observation.

**Divergence, not convergence.** Two organizations that look similar at the start of AI adoption should grow more different over time, not more similar. This distinguishes the asymmetric-attractor theory from simpler alternatives: a theory in which AI is uniformly faster predicts everyone gets faster at similar rates; a theory in which AI quality merely varies predicts a roughly normal spread of outcomes. The asymmetric-attractor theory predicts bifurcation — two distinct clusters, with the gap widening. The DORA finding that AI amplifies the gap between strong and weak organizations rather than democratizing capability is exactly this signature, observed at scale.[^dora]

**Invisible early, irreversible late.** Bistable systems have the property that the regime is determined by where you are in state space, not by current trajectory. An organization can be heading toward the decay attractor while currently looking healthy: velocity is fine, but the substrate is degrading at a rate that becomes visible only later. Conversely, an organization can look slow while heading toward the self-sustaining attractor: substrate investment is consuming time that would otherwise produce features, but the returns will compound. So early signals — six-month velocity, current throughput — should be poor predictors of long-term outcomes, while late signals — substrate coherence at eighteen to twenty-four months — should be excellent ones. The METR randomized trial sharpens this: experienced developers on mature codebases were measurably *slower* with AI while believing they were faster — direct evidence that the felt sense of velocity is an unreliable signal about the underlying trajectory.[^metr]

**Specific symptoms of the externally-propped decay regime.** Organizations in the decay regime should exhibit a coherent cluster of symptoms: agent context that drifts from the actual state of the codebase; conventions that contradict each other across recent changes; evaluation suites that grow in size but lose signal as they fill with brittle tests; an increasing fraction of human time spent re-correcting agent output rather than reviewing genuinely new work; codebases that grow faster than the team's understanding of them; onboarding — of new humans or new agent configurations — that gets harder over time rather than easier. These cluster because they share a cause: the substrate is being relied on faster than it is maintained. The independent measurement here is striking — GitClear's analysis of over 200 million changed lines found code duplication roughly quadrupling and copy-paste overtaking refactoring for the first time as AI adoption rose, while Stanford's study across roughly a hundred thousand developers found that net productivity gains shrink or go negative once rework is subtracted, with the worst results in exactly the brownfield, high-complexity setting where substrate matters most.[^gitclear][^stanford]

**Specific behaviors of the self-sustaining regime.** Organizations in the self-sustaining regime should exhibit a different coherent cluster: the description tape treated as a first-class deliverable, reviewed alongside code; review questions oriented toward substrate quality ("does this change strengthen or weaken the tape?") rather than only feature correctness; evaluation suites actively curated, with tests deleted as well as added, growing in signal rather than volume; agent configurations improved on an ongoing basis rather than set up once and left to rot; onboarding that gets *faster* over time because the substrate does more of the teaching. These cluster because the organization treats the substrate as load-bearing and invests in maintaining it.

The contemporary evidence supports the theory in two ways. The bifurcation is empirically visible — two qualitatively different kinds of AI-era organization coexist under the same label of "we use AI for engineering." And the symptom clusters reported in each kind match the predictions closely. If organizations were converging instead of diverging, or if the reported symptoms did not cluster as predicted, the theory would be in trouble.

One further observation supports the asymmetric framing specifically. Organizations in the decay regime rarely collapse outright. They persist, often for years, at a survival floor of functioning-but-degraded output — exactly what the theory predicts, because the decay attractor is internally unstable but externally propped. As long as external forcing holds them above the dissolution point, they remain in business indefinitely. They produce software. The software is increasingly incoherent. They do not die. They simply stop being the kind of organization that could cross the threshold without rebuilding.

These data points are evidence, not proof. The strong test is whether the predicted divergence continues over the next several years and whether the symptom clusters hold across a wider sample. The theory is falsifiable in a specific way: if the bifurcation reverses, or if the two clusters turn out to be statistical artifacts rather than structurally distinct regimes, the theory is wrong. Right now, neither failure mode appears to be occurring. It is worth flagging one caveat in the data: DORA's 2025 throughput finding reversed its 2024 result, and METR's trial used early-2025 tooling on a small sample — both consistent with a field still learning to use the tape, but a reminder that the empirical picture is young.

## Implications and Open Questions

The most consequential question an engineering leader can now ask is not "how fast are we shipping?" but "is our description tape compounding or decaying?" Velocity is downstream of substrate coherence. The leading indicator of long-term outcomes is whether each loop leaves the tape sharper or duller than it found it. This is a different question than engineering leadership was trained to ask, and most evaluation frameworks, hiring rubrics, and org structures have not absorbed it.

The theory does not prescribe what to do. It is not an operations manual. But it makes certain questions unavoidable.

**Does the description tape exist at all, in any load-bearing form?** Many organizations have CLAUDE.md files and conventions documents that are decorative — written once and ignored. The concrete test is the closure audit from earlier: list what your agents read to build, and for each item name the loop step that keeps it true. Any item whose only answer is "a human remembers to" is a hole. A real description tape is one whose loss would cripple the engineering loop. If an organization can lose its tape with little effect on its work, it does not have a tape; it has documentation.

**What is the trajectory of the tape — not its state, but its derivative?** Is it getting more coherent loop over loop, or less? Is it reviewed and edited as part of the normal flow of work, or stagnant while the codebase moves on without it? This is the most reliable predictor of which regime the organization is in.

**What fraction of human hours should go to substrate versus construction?** If substrate is capital formation and construction is increasingly delegated, the pre-AI default of nearly all hours on construction is no longer correct. The right ratio is unknown and probably organization-specific, but the default has shifted, and organizations that have not adjusted are likely under-investing in the substrate that determines their trajectory.

**Should hiring and evaluation change?** If the role has reclassified, the criteria should too. The skills that mattered most pre-AI — construction throughput, debugging speed, holding system context in working memory — are increasingly automated. The skills that matter most now — clear system description, eval design, architectural judgment, recognizing when implicit knowledge should be codified — are not yet standard parts of most hiring rubrics. Organizations that keep optimizing for the pre-AI profile will accumulate engineers well-suited to work that is disappearing.

Several questions the theory does not resolve.

*How do you measure q in practice?* The theory operates on the behavior of r, not the absolute value of q — by design, since the predictions are about regime, not magnitude. But that leaves practitioners without a way to quantify their position. Real metrics for substrate coherence are empirical work the theory motivates but does not perform.

*Is r actually constant, or state-dependent?* The model treats r as a fixed multiplier for tractability. In reality it almost certainly depends on the current state of D: the same practices that yield r > 1 in a clean codebase may yield r < 1 in a tangled one, because the substrate's degradation interferes with the maintenance work itself. That feedback loop complicates the dynamics and may soften the threshold.

*How sharp is the threshold in real systems?* RAF theory shows a sharp transition: closure either holds or it does not. Engineering reality may be softer, with gradients near the boundary and metastable intermediate states. The sharp-threshold prediction is the strongest form of the claim; weaker forms — a region of unstable transition between two regimes — would still preserve most of the structure.

*What is the engineering analog of the food set?* In chemistry, autocatalytic networks still require some minimal external inputs — the food set — to operate. In engineering the analog is probably human judgment and architectural taste: a self-sustaining system still needs human input, but only at the level of high-leverage substrate decisions, not construction. This is the same boundary Naur drew — the residue of the "theory of the program" that does not externalize even now. How large that residue is, and whether it shrinks as models improve, is the deepest open question the framework raises.

*How does external forcing interact with internal substrate quality?* The model treats them as separate, but they interact: customer pressure and market position shape which kinds of substrate decay get tolerated and which get fixed. An organization in a forgiving market can decay further without consequence than one in a punishing market. The separation is a useful first-order approximation, not the full picture.

*How does the labor reclassification propagate?* The theory predicts work has reclassified from labor to capital formation. It does not predict how that will move through university programs, hiring practices, compensation, immigration policy, or the broader industry. These second-order consequences are likely significant and outside the theory's scope.

The theory is presented for clarity, not certainty. It will be wrong in places and incomplete in others. The mathematics is suggestive rather than quantitatively predictive, and the empirical evidence is currently consistent with the theory but not yet sufficient to validate it strongly. Better theories may emerge.

But the architectural shift it describes is real. Engineering organizations have acquired a description tape — a substrate that did not exist, in any structurally meaningful sense, in pre-AI engineering. The asymmetric attractor structure is observable. The labor reclassification is already underway. The bifurcation between organizations that have crossed the threshold and organizations that have not is visible to anyone working in the field.

Understanding the architecture is the first step toward operating inside it deliberately. The questions follow from the architecture; the actions follow from the questions. The theory does not prescribe the actions, but it makes clear what kind of system is now being operated, what kind of work that operation requires, and what kind of consequences follow from doing the work well or poorly.

Software engineering has crossed a threshold that, until recently, was not crossable. The work that follows from that crossing is different in kind from what came before. The organizations that recognize this will operate inside the new architecture on purpose. The organizations that do not will keep operating inside the old one — externally propped, slowly decaying, increasingly incoherent — until external forcing fails to sustain them.

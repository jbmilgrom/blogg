In 1985, Peter Naur argued that a program is not its source code but a theory: the understanding, held in the minds of its builders, of how the code maps to the world and why it is shaped the way it is. The theory cannot be reconstructed from the code and its documentation, and when the team holding it disperses, the program dies — it still runs, but "demands for modifications cannot be intelligently answered."[^naur] Forty years of dead codebases and orphaned modules have confirmed him.

Naur's constraint was never about the theory itself. It was about where the theory had to live. Heads are a specific kind of storage: rival (a senior engineer's attention goes to one person at a time), non-accumulating (knowledge transferred by conversation is re-paid each time, not banked), and lossy (it walks out the door). Knowledge stored that way cannot compound, so every organization bought its theory as labor, over and over, and decay was the default at any level of effort or talent. AI is the first technology that lets the theory live somewhere else: in artifacts that machines read, act on, and help maintain. Make the theory of your program executable, and decay stops being the default. Decline, and you keep the decay — with more horsepower behind it.

The industry is currently running this experiment, and the early results split. Google's 2025 DORA report, surveying roughly a hundred thousand practitioners, found that AI _amplifies_ what an organization already is, widening the gap between the strong and the struggling.[^dora] The degrading half cannot see itself: in a randomized trial by METR, experienced developers were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they had been roughly 20% faster.[^metr] "Software factory" and "loop engineering" are the buzzwords of the season, and the practice literature is full of genuinely good mechanics — conventions files, harness configuration — but it offers no account of why tending the loop compounds while merely adopting the tools does not, or why identical technology is splitting the field in two.[^genre] The answer to both questions is Naur's.

## The Theory, and Why Decay Was Inherent

The theory is what changing code safely consumes. A codebase is formal text, just like the compiler beneath it; the difference is that you have to keep changing it, and change requires what the text does not contain — which conventions are load-bearing, why the architecture is shaped this way, what breaks three modules away. Every modification spends theory, and modification never stops, because the world the program serves keeps moving.

Because the theory lived in heads, spending it meant paying people to hold and transmit it, and the payment never banked. This is the root of the oldest observations in software engineering. Brooks called maintenance "an entropy-increasing process, and even its most skillful execution only delays the subsidence of the system into unfixable obsolescence."[^brooks] Brooks's law — adding people to a late project makes it later — is the same constraint seen from the staffing side: the binding resource was never hands but shared theory, and capacity does not substitute for it. New people consume theory from the people who have it before contributing any; the transmission is the cost.

## Turning Theory Into Code

The most effective entropy-fighting in software history has been converting fragments of the theory into executable form. Static types are invariants the compiler enforces on everyone, forever. A test banks the theory behind a fix — this must stay true — and catches its regression for the rest of the codebase's life. Linters and CI are the style guide and the process document, enforcing themselves. Infrastructure-as-code is the most recent large crossing, and every reader watched it: runbooks — descriptions of production kept true by nobody — became Terraform plans and Kubernetes operators, executable, versioned, reviewed, and nobody argues for going back. The Linux kernel is the longest-running demonstration: its development loop produced git when its version control was pulled out from under it, `checkpatch.pl` to make its style rules executable, Coccinelle to write tree-wide modification knowledge in a formal language — and the kernel has survived thirty-five years of continuous modification by thousands of rotating contributors in large part because it pushed more of its theory into executable form than any project before it.

These fragments compounded for a simple reason: an executable artifact works without its author present and replicates at no cost, so knowledge in that form is written once and enforced on every future change.[^romer] The limit was the price of crossing. Every fragment cost a hand-built formalism — a type system, a test harness, Terraform itself — so only knowledge worth a program crossed, and the judgment-laden bulk of the theory never did. The kernel's `MAINTAINERS` file carries an explicit `Orphan` status for subsystems whose theory-holder left; the formalisms could not save what they could not express.

## Culture Carried the Rest

What could not be formalized got a social system instead. Documentation, design docs, code review, mentorship, onboarding curricula: engineering culture is the apparatus a team builds to transmit the unformalizable part of its theory from head to head. The most admired engineering cultures are the most disciplined versions of this apparatus — Google's company-wide readability process, Jane Street's early practice of senior traders reading every line entering the core systems, NASA's shuttle group producing 2,500 pages of specification for a 6,366-line change.[^google][^janestreet][^nasa]

All of it worked, and none of it compounded. Knowledge transmitted person-to-person is re-paid on every transmission; documentation drifts because a description that nothing executes is a description that nothing keeps true. NASA is the price-ceiling experiment: with effectively unlimited process budget, you could buy near-perfect correctness — and velocity still did not rise release over release, because the spend was consumed, not invested. Culture was the best available answer to Naur's constraint. It was never an exit from it.

## The Remainder Becomes Executable

An LLM agent acts on informal text, which removes the price of crossing. A convention written in prose is now a convention an agent applies. A recorded demonstration is now something a toolchain builds from. The judgment-laden remainder — the conventions, the rationale, the accumulated sense of what works in this codebase — no longer needs a hand-built formalism, because prose has become a formalism. The shape of engineering work changes accordingly, and it is worth writing down as a signature:

$$\text{Loop}(\text{task},\ \{\text{description},\ \text{codebase}\}) \rightarrow \{\text{description}',\ \text{codebase}'\}$$

The description of the codebase — its theory, externalized — enters every task as input and returns as output. Construction updates the codebase; the lesson of the task, when there is one, updates the description. Both are versioned, both are reviewed, and the next task reads the sharper description.

This is not hypothetical; it is how our repository at [Docflow Labs](https://docflowlabs.com) runs. The description layer is a set of markdown skills, conventions files, process-requirements documents, and build gates that agents read on every task. When a production automation failed because generated code had assumed a rich combo-box widget where the page had a native `<select>`, the fix was one line and agent-authored — and the durable change was a colleague's same-day commit hardening the build gates: every selector must now trace to a verified source, and the control's _type_ must be confirmed against that source. That class of bug can no longer ship, because the thing that builds our automations reads the gate on every build. The lesson landed in an artifact, not in a person.

The signature has a price, and the price is adjudication. A compiler's translation is correct by construction; nobody reviews the emitted assembly. An agent's translation is correct only by verification — nothing guarantees the built thing means what the description meant — so the loop grows checks at every stage: mechanical gates, trace-grounded audits, review agents, and humans at approval. Closure — the state where the loop maintains every description it consumes — therefore cannot mean "no humans." The workable definition: for every description the loop reads, some step _of the loop_ keeps it true. A human adjudicating at a gate is such a step; a description that stays current only because someone remembers to update it is not. By that definition our own loop is partially closed: the banking mechanism exists, but the decision that a lesson deserves to become an instruction still fires only when a person notices. And the price cannot be skipped: the research on self-updating agent contexts exists largely because unadjudicated self-update measurably degrades — the ACE line of work calls the failure mode context collapse, and its remedy is a curation step before anything is banked.[^ace]

A loop with this signature has a name. Von Neumann showed that self-reproduction requires a description read two ways — interpreted as instructions to build, and copied as data to propagate — and a machine organized around that doubleness: constructor, copier, controller, tape.[^vn] Agents construct, version control copies, the workflow coordinates, and the executable description is the tape. The identity supplies a test that "documentation" never had: if your organization could lose the artifact with little effect on its work, it is documentation, theory living elsewhere; if losing it would cripple the loop, it is a tape.[^futamura]

## Pointed at Code Alone, the Entropy Remains

The most common way to adopt AI is to point agents at the code and leave the theory in heads. This raises capacity inside Naur's regime; it does not exit the regime. Brooks's law generalizes directly: adding agents to an entropy-dominated codebase is adding people to a late project — except agents add change volume at machine speed against a theory that is standing still, so incoherence accumulates faster than the pre-AI world ever produced it. The failure mode of the AI era is not slower decay interrupted by tools; it is accelerated decay that feels like progress, because construction throughput — the thing perception tracks — genuinely rises while the description of the system quietly falls behind it.

The negative results in the industry data describe this population. METR's developers were slower with AI while feeling faster; Stanford's measurements across roughly a hundred thousand developers find rework eating most of the raw gains, with the worst results in exactly the brownfield, high-complexity settings where theory matters most.[^stanford] GitClear's finding that code duplication roughly quadrupled as AI adoption rose[^gitclear] needs one distinction before it counts: duplication is benign when the sameness among copies lives in the description and the copies are regenerable output, and malignant when the copies are the source of truth and their sameness is recorded nowhere — theory that exists but is written down in zero places. What tips GitClear's data toward the malignant reading is that refactoring, the act of moving sameness into the description, declined as duplication rose. None of these studies contradicts the compounding loop; they document its absence where no one built one. And the diagnostic follows from the mechanism: watch the derivative of your description — sharper or duller than one loop ago — not your throughput, which METR shows will actively mislead you.

## The Relocation of Labor

If construction is delegated and the description compounds, the highest-return hour a human can spend moves from the product to the description. AI reads and writes English, so it belongs on both sides of that work — drafting the skills, proposing the updates, surfacing the lessons in root-cause documents — with humans adjudicating what becomes instruction. An hour spent fixing a bug is consumed by that bug. An hour spent on the gate that follows the bug is invested: every future build, by any agent, reads it. The knowledge stops being rival the moment it stops living in a head.

Our commit record confirms the relocation. Over the past year the repository accumulated about 4,600 commits, roughly 60% authored by agents, over five hundred of them by autonomous bots with no human driving. The instruction layer tells the sharper story: of the 345 commits that touched it, five came from autonomous agents, and the other 340 trace to a human deciding the machine needed better instructions. Agents write the product; humans write the loop. The relocation's unfinished edge is the trigger — ours still fires on human memory — and the tool ecosystem confirms that this is the frontier: an entire category now automates lesson-banking into per-developer memory databases,[^tools] which recreates the original problem one level up, because the banked lessons are unversioned and unshared — theory in an agent's head instead of a human's, outside version control, the same mistake I flagged in January about self-healed selectors living outside git.[^prior] The designs that work converge on one shape: automatically propose, humanly or mechanically adjudicate, bank into versioned artifacts.

## The Audit and the Residue

The practical takeaway fits in two questions. Does an artifact exist whose loss would cripple your engineering loop — a tape — or only documentation nobody would miss for a week? And is it sharper or duller than it was a loop ago — when production teaches a lesson, does the lesson land in an artifact agents read, or in a person? For everything your agents read, name the loop step that keeps it true. Every answer of the form "someone remembers to" is a hole, and each hole is a place where you are still paying labor rates for what could be capital.

Something real remains on the human side, and the theory says what it is. The loop consumes judgment — which lessons matter, whether a proposed instruction is right, what the system should become. That residue is Naur's boundary drawn one layer up: the theory of the _loop_, and it does not currently externalize. It sits at the two stations a control system reserves for its operator — the reference input and the approval gate — which in our shop look like a merge button, an exception queue, and a colleague reading a production failure and deciding it deserves to become a rule.

Naur said a program is a theory held by its builders, and that when they disperse, it dies. He was right for forty years, and every dead codebase confirmed him. What changed is not that the theory became unnecessary but that it acquired a second place to live — one that machines read, version control copies, and the loop itself helps maintain. The program used to die with its team. Now that is a choice an organization makes, one commit at a time, about where its lessons go.

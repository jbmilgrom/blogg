Last month one of our browser automations failed in production. The code assumed that a dropdown was the same rich combo-box widget frequently used across the portal; on this particular page, the control was a native `<select>` tag.

Even though the fix was only one line and the bug was flagged and fixed programmatically via an agentic bug detection, investigation, implementation, review and verification pipeline, it was still evidence of a larger issue in our software engineering stack. The same day, a colleague landed `docs(skills): harden build-tool gates — verified selectors + migration check` in order to update the machine-read instructions our coding agents build from. The build process now refuses any selector that doesn't trace to a verified source, including live exploration of the real page, a captured trace, or an explicit human handoff, and requires the control's _type_ to be confirmed against that source. That class of bug can no longer ship in the first place because the thing that builds our automations reads it on every build.

The same week, many routine fixes were authored by `docflow-investigator[bot]`, an agent with its own GitHub account that is woken by failed runs and opens pull requests with root-cause analyses attached — a step of our pipeline that requires an analysis of cause, and human approval, before implementation begins. Its commits that week included `fix: wait for AJAX results in the Assigned To dropdown before selecting` and `fix: use DST-aware start-of-day in tracking retry dedup`.[^redact]

The division of labor in those two paragraphs holds across our whole repository, aptly named `meta-tools` (if I may say so myself). Humans keep the instruction layer for themselves — the agent-facing conventions, skills, build gates, and runtime that direct all that construction — and delegate the product loop, from detection through investigation, implementation, review and verification, to agents. Over the past year the repository accumulated about 4,600 commits, roughly 60% of them authored by agents — over five hundred by autonomous bots with no human driving. The split is sharpest at the instruction layer. Of the 345 commits that touched it, five came from autonomous agents; the other 340 trace to a human deciding the machine needed better instructions.

Meanwhile, the industry-wide data on AI-assisted engineering shows something strange. Google's 2025 DORA report, surveying roughly a hundred thousand practitioners, found that AI _amplifies_ what an organization already is, widening the gap between the strong and the struggling.[^dora] Notwithstanding the same models and tools, some teams accelerate, while others measurably degrade. The degrading half is real and cannot see itself: in a randomized trial by METR, experienced developers were 19% slower with AI tools while believing they had been roughly 20% faster.[^metr] Somebody is compounding and somebody is rotting, on identical technology.

"Software factory" and "loop engineering" are, as I write this, the buzzwords of the season. Consultancies publish maturity models, vendors sell the tooling, and the practice literature is full of genuinely good advice about conventions files and harness configuration.[^genre] I have no quarrel with the label — the phenomenon is real. What the discourse is missing is a theory: why tending the loop _compounds_ rather than merely helping, why identical tooling is splitting the field into two diverging populations rather than lifting it, what any of this has to do with the previous fifty years of software, and how to tell — before the trend lines make it obvious — which population your organization is in. Those are the questions this post tries to answer.

The answer, compressed: AI improves software engineering twice. The visible effect is construction — agents write code, throughput rises. That gain is large, immediate, and _linear_: each feature built is a return collected once. The structural effect is that the same capability makes the knowledge of _how software gets built_ — conventions, doctrine, judgment — executable, which turns the engineering loop from something maintained by human labor into something you can invest in. Construction gains add. Loop gains compound, because the loop amplifies the very attention that improves it. And because that amplification runs in both directions, the second effect is also what splits the field. The result, for the first time in engineering's history, is a real exit from decay as the default condition — an exit that is offered, not granted.

## Banked or Taxed

Fifty years of software history sort every piece of engineering knowledge into one of two fates: banked or taxed. The banked knowledge is the tooling stack, and it is best read not as a set of products but as capitalized skills. A compiler is the skill of translation, written down in executable form; git is the kernel community's change-management practice, made into an artifact; a framework is a thousand solved design problems, frozen for reuse; a test suite is the accumulated knowledge of what must stay true. Knowledge in this form has two properties nothing head-shaped ever had: it executes without its author present, and it replicates at zero marginal cost.[^romer] And because software is a tool for making software, the artifacts compose — each layer is written with the accumulated leverage of every layer below it and then joins the stock.[^arthur] Outputs re-enter as inputs, which is the definition of compounding, and it is why a single developer today wields leverage that would have taken a department in 1980: not smarter people, but fifty years of banked knowledge underneath them.

Zero marginal cost covers replication, though, and not upkeep. Banked knowledge is capital, and capital depreciates: the environment drifts underneath every artifact — specs evolve, hardware changes, dependencies churn — and use itself accretes constraints, because with enough users every observable behavior becomes something somebody depends on.[^hyrum] So every layer of the compounding stack carries a standing maintenance crew, and the crew's own knowledge is head-stored: V8 runs on a small priesthood whose onboarding is famously hard, and where no crew is funded at all, the stack fails from below — OpenSSL before Heartbleed, log4j, the xz backdoor's lone overwhelmed maintainer. The two ledgers of software history are one system: reuse compounds only because someone continuously pays a maintenance tax underneath it.

The tax has a precise source. Deployed software must keep changing — Brooks called maintenance "an entropy-increasing process, and even its most skillful execution only delays the subsidence of the system into unfixable obsolescence"[^brooks] — and changing formal text safely requires something the text does not contain: the conventions, the rationale, the sense of what breaks what. Peter Naur called this the _theory_ of the program and argued, in 1985, that it lives only in the minds of the people who built it; that it cannot be reconstructed from code and documentation alone; and that when the team disperses, the program dies — it still runs, but "demands for modifications cannot be intelligently answered."[^naur]

The theory was stored in heads, and heads are a specific kind of storage: rival (a senior engineer's attention goes to one person at a time), non-accumulating (knowledge transferred by conversation is re-paid each time, not banked), and lossy (it walks out the door). Stored that way, the knowledge that governs change could only ever be labor. The storage medium, and not any lack of effort, is what kept it labor. Documentation was the obvious attempted fix, and it failed on schedule in every organization, for a reason worth stating precisely: a description that nothing executes is a description that nothing keeps true. Docs drift, and the drift is silent, because no build breaks when the architecture diagram becomes fiction. (Human-executed documentation _can_ be load-bearing — aviation checklists are — but only where a compliance regime supplies the enforcement, which is labor again.) The most impressive engineering cultures on record are best understood as the most effective patching systems ever built for this hole: Google's company-wide readability process, Jane Street's senior traders reading every line entering the core systems, NASA's shuttle group producing 2,500 pages of specification for a 6,366-line change.[^google][^janestreet][^nasa] All of it worked, and none of it compounded. NASA is the price-ceiling experiment: with effectively unlimited process budget, you could buy near-perfect correctness — and velocity still didn't rise loop over loop, because the spend was consumed, not invested.

Linux looks like the counterexample and is actually the boundary marker. The kernel has been continuously modified for thirty-five years by thousands of rotating contributors and got better — because its development loop kept converting its own process knowledge into executable form. It produced git, the loop building its own version control in 2005 when its previous one was pulled out from under it. It produced `checkpatch.pl`, the coding-style document made executable; Coccinelle semantic patches, tree-wide modification knowledge written in a formal language; kernel selftests; syzkaller, which discovers bugs and files them back into the loop. Git's own repository shows the same habit: nearly every fix banks a regression test, so the suite is loop-produced process knowledge that makes future modification safer. This is real self-maintenance of the loop's own instructions — decades before anyone said "agentic" — and attention invested there banked: a test written once catches its regression forever.

The practice had a price and a boundary. Each item crossed into executable form cost a hand-written program — checkpatch is real engineering, Coccinelle was a research project — so the economics confined the practice to a sliver. And the judgment-laden bulk never crossed at any price: the kernel's MAINTAINERS file has an explicit `Orphan` status for subsystems whose theory-holder left; succession anxiety has been a standing community concern for two decades; onboarding stayed famously brutal; and throughput scaled roughly linearly with (corporate-funded) headcount — the signature of labor, not capital. Linux pressed against the boundary harder than anyone, which is why it shows precisely where the boundary sat.

Operations knowledge crossed the same boundary within living memory, and every reader watched it happen. Runbooks were the purest case of documentation's failure mode: descriptions of how production works, kept true by nobody, consulted mid-incident in the hope they still corresponded to reality. Then infrastructure-as-code gave that knowledge a formalism to cross into, and it became Terraform plans and Kubernetes operators — executable, versioned, reviewed, load-bearing. The knowledge that once lived in the heads of whoever had been paged the most now sits in artifacts a machine applies and a pull request can change, and nobody argues for going back. The crossing still carried the pre-AI price — someone had to build Terraform — but it demonstrates the pattern this essay turns on: knowledge that finds an executable formalism stops being a tax and starts being capital.

That is the boundary in full. Engineering knowledge compounds where it can live in an executable artifact, and taxes where it can only live in a person — and for fifty years, the knowledge that governs change, the theory itself, was the class with no formalism to cross into. It was the one input to software production that never became capital.

## The Description Becomes Executable

What AI changes is the cost and the reach of the crossing. An LLM agent acts on informal text, so knowledge no longer needs a hand-built formalism to become executable — you write the convention in prose and the agent applies it; you record a demonstration and the toolchain builds from it. The class of knowledge that never had a formalism — conventions, review doctrine, exception-handling policy, the accumulated judgment about what works in this codebase — finally has one. In our repository that layer is a set of markdown skills, conventions files, process-requirements documents, and build gates that agents read on every task — including the gate from the opening of this post, which contains, verbatim, the instruction that a lambda is not built until "documenting intent is not building" is satisfied by mechanical checks: grep for stub markers, at least one green run against the live system, every dropdown constant diffed against the live option set.

Executability is also what keeps the description true. Our roadmap generator operates under a written rule — "never invent selectors, never guess attribute values"; anything not extracted from the recording is marked `[NOT IN RECORDING]` — and the rule holds not because anyone re-reads it but because artifacts that violate it produce automations that fail runs, visibly. When the description is load-bearing, drift has consequences, and consequences are what documentation never had.

## At the Price of Adjudication

The price of building from informal descriptions is verification, and it restructures the loop. A compiler's translation is correct by construction; nobody reviews the emitted assembly. An agent's translation is correct only by _adjudication_ — nothing guarantees the built thing means what the description meant — so a loop with agents in it grows verification at every stage: the anti-fabrication rules, the mechanical gates, trace-grounded audits of what actually happened in production, review agents on every pull request. This is why closure — the state where the loop maintains every description it consumes — cannot honestly be defined as "no humans." The workable definition: for every description the loop reads, some step _of the loop_ keeps it true. A human adjudicating at a gate is such a step. A description that stays current only because a particular human remembers to update it is not a loop step; it is the old regime, however good the human's memory. Closure comes in degrees, and the degree is auditable.

Our own loop, audited by that definition, is partially closed. We have the banking mechanism — the gates, the skills, an investigation agent that drafts root-cause documents, even PR-reviewed process-requirements documents the investigation agent proposes updates to — but the decision that a production lesson deserves to become an instruction still fires only when a person notices and cares. The _trigger_ is human memory, and by the definition above, that is a hole. The audit shows exactly which parts are closed and which are not.

Skipping the adjudication has been tried, and the result has been measured. The research frontier maintains agent contexts as "evolving playbooks" substantially because naive self-updating contexts _degrade_ — the ACE line of work calls the failure mode context collapse — and its remedy is a curation step before anything is banked.[^ace] Unadjudicated closure rots fast. Adjudication is not overhead on the loop; it is the difference between closure as an asset and closure as an accelerant of decay.

## The Description Tape

The system that results when the loop holds has a recognizable architecture. Agents read a description and build from it. Version control copies the description forward, unchanged, from cycle to cycle. The loop's own activity — investigation, review, gate-hardening — produces the next version of the description it will read. A system that reads a description to build, copies that description as data, and produces the description that drives it is von Neumann's architecture for self-reproducing machines,[^vn] and the correspondence is structural rather than metaphorical.

Von Neumann's insight, worked out in the 1940s, was that self-reproduction requires a specific separation: a constructor that reads instructions and builds; a copier that duplicates the instructions _without interpreting them_; a controller coordinating the two; and the instructions themselves — the description tape. The tape is read two ways — interpreted as instructions to build, copied as data to propagate — and this doubleness is what makes self-reproduction possible without infinite regress. The two readings are the two capital properties from earlier in this post: executable without a human interpreter, and copyable at zero marginal cost.

The mapping to an AI-era engineering organization is close but I will not call it exact. Agents are the constructor; git is the copier; the workflow — tickets, review, deployment — is the controller; the externalized description is the tape. Two honest disanalogies: the organization reproduces itself across _generations_, not in space — no second org pops out — and the constructor is bought, not built; the models arrive from outside, part of the environment, along with the residue of human judgment the system consumes. What the identity buys, despite the caveats, is a bright line that "documentation" never had: read the tape as the thing the system reproduces itself from, and you get a test. If your organization could lose the artifact with little effect on its work, it isn't a tape — it's documentation, inert in exactly Naur's sense, theory living elsewhere. If losing it would cripple the loop, it is a tape.[^futamura]

## Two Basins, One Allocation Decision

The pre-AI development loop had one moving part. Write it as a recurrence and the codebase evolves under a fixed process: the next codebase is the process applied to the current one, and the process itself — tools, conventions, theory — changes on a slower timeline the loop doesn't see, identically for you and your competitors. The AI-era loop with closure has two moving parts, jointly:

$$(D_{i+1},\; cb_{i+1}) = F(D_i,\; cb_i)$$

The description shapes what gets built; what gets built — lessons, failures, audits — reshapes the description. Define r as the ratio of description quality across one cycle: above one, the tape left the loop sharper than it entered; below one, duller. I won't pretend q is measurable today; the claims here are about the _behavior_ of r, not its value. r is a rate of return on the loop itself.

The reason this produces two diverging populations rather than a spread of outcomes is that the return is state-dependent, with feedback in both directions. Invest attention in the loop and the loop amplifies your next hour of investment: agents draft the skills, surface the lessons in root-cause documents, audit the tape for contradictions. Better tape also means faster, cleaner construction, which frees more attention for the tape. That is a self-reinforcing climb. Neglect the loop and the feedback reverses at a speed the pre-AI world never exhibited: agents amplify construction throughput against a description that is quietly degrading, so incoherence accumulates at machine speed rather than human speed, and the cost of catching up grows with every cycle you defer it. Pre-AI organizations decayed at the rate humans could type. An open loop with agents in it is _accelerated_ rot. One dynamic, two basins, and the separatrix runs through an allocation decision: whether some of the attention the construction dividend freed up gets reinvested in the machinery that produced it.

The industry data fits this picture, with care about what it can and cannot show. DORA's amplification finding — AI widening the gap between strong and struggling organizations — is what bistability looks like from the outside, but I'll claim only consistency, not proof: a continuous amplifier on pre-existing org quality would also widen gaps, and the discriminating evidence would be bimodality that sharpens over time, which no one has cleanly shown yet.[^dora] What the theory does confidently reframe is the negative results. METR's developers — slower with AI while feeling faster — and Stanford's finding that rework eats most measured gains[^stanford] are measurements of the _open-loop population_: construction amplified, description untended, exactly the regime the model says should feel fast and measure slow. GitClear's data — code duplication roughly quadrupling as AI adoption rose[^gitclear] — needs one more distinction before it can be read, because duplication is evidence of rot only under the pre-AI cost model, where every copy had to be found and maintained by hand. Duplication is benign when the sameness among copies lives in the description — a template, a generator, a shared abstraction — and the copies are regenerable output; compilers have always duplicated this way, and our own repository stamps out a fleet of similar automations by design. It is malignant when the copies are the source of truth and their sameness is recorded nowhere, because then the relationship among seventeen implementations of one policy is itself tacit knowledge — a hole in the description — and agent-speed modification turns quiet divergence into accelerated incoherence. GitClear's duplication count cannot distinguish the two cases; what tips its data toward the malignant reading is the accompanying finding that refactoring — the act of moving sameness _into_ the description — declined as duplication rose. None of these studies contradicts compounding in closed loops; they document its absence where closure is absent. The genre's "3–5x productivity" framing has this exactly backwards — the first-order claim to check about an organization adopting AI is not its throughput but the sign of r. And the theory is falsifiable on its own terms: it predicts the two populations diverge over the coming years, that early velocity is a poor predictor of trajectory while substrate coherence at eighteen months is a good one, and that the symptom clusters — evals losing signal, conventions contradicting recent changes, onboarding getting harder instead of easier — travel together, because they share a cause.

## The Relocation of Labor

A second implication follows, for human work. If construction is delegated and loop gains compound, the highest-return hour a human can spend moves from the product to the loop: an hour spent fixing a bug is consumed by that bug, while an hour spent on the gate that follows the bug is invested — every future build, by any agent, on any task, reads it. The knowledge stops being rival the moment it stops living in a head. Our commit record confirms the prediction. The autonomous bots author hundreds of commits and virtually never touch the instruction layer — five commits out of their seven hundred — while the other 340 instruction-layer changes this year trace to a human deciding the machine needed better instructions, most executed through agent-driven sessions with a human directing, some typed by hand. Agents write the product; humans write the loop. The relocation is not humans replaced, and not humans doing what they did before with better autocomplete, but human attention concentrating on the layer whose returns compound.

The unfinished edge of the relocation is the trigger — ours still fires on human memory — and the ecosystem has noticed the same hole, which tells you where the frontier is. An entire tool category now exists to automate the banking trigger — claude-mem, Mem0, the coding agents' native auto-memory features — and the popular design shares one property: lessons are extracted automatically and banked into a per-developer database.[^tools] That design automates the trigger by giving up the two properties that made banked knowledge capital in the first place. The database is unversioned (you cannot diff, review, or revert what your agent believes), and it is unshared by default (your agent's lessons are not your teammate's). It is tacit knowledge recreated one level up — living in an agent's head instead of a human's — and it repeats a mistake I flagged in January about cached selectors living outside git: state that steers production behavior, outside version control.[^prior] The designs that work have converged on the same shape from different directions: automatically _propose_, humanly or mechanically _adjudicate_, bank into _versioned_ artifacts. Load-bearing, adjudicated, versioned — tools will come and go, but those three criteria are the test.

## The Audit and the Residue

The practical takeaway fits in two questions. First: does the tape exist, by the loss test — is there an artifact whose destruction would cripple your engineering loop, or just documentation nobody would miss for a week? Second, the derivative rather than the state: is the tape sharper or duller than it was a loop ago — when production teaches a lesson, does the lesson land in an artifact agents read, or in a person? For everything your agents read, name the loop step that keeps it true; every answer of the form "someone remembers to" is a hole, and the holes are where your organization is still paying labor rates for what could be capital. That question — not this quarter's velocity, which METR suggests will actively mislead you — is the leading indicator of which basin you're in.

Something real remains on the human side, and the theory says what it is. Von Neumann's machine still consumes raw material from its environment; ours consumes judgment — which lessons matter, what the system should become, whether a proposed instruction is right. That residue is Naur's boundary drawn one layer up: the theory of the _loop_, not of the program, and it does not currently externalize. It sits at the two stations a control system reserves for its operator — the reference input and the approval gate — which in our shop look like a merge button, an exception queue, and a colleague reading a production failure and deciding it deserves to become a rule.

Naur said a program is a theory held by its builders, and that when they disperse, it dies. He was right for forty years, and every dead codebase and orphaned subsystem confirmed him. What changed is not that the theory became unnecessary but that it acquired a second place to live — one that machines read, version control copies, and the loop itself helps maintain. The program used to die with its team. Now that's a choice an organization makes, one commit at a time, about where its lessons go.
